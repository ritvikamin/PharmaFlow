## 4. Likely questions and answers

**Why a star schema?**
Analytics questions are "total or average of an event, grouped by something": sales by region, shipments by plant. A star schema keeps events in fact tables and descriptions in small dimension tables, so those queries are simple joins plus GROUP BY, and descriptions are stored once. A fully normalized transactional design would need more joins for the same questions. The cost is some repeated keys in the facts, which is fine for reporting.

**What is the grain of your fact tables?**
fact_sales and fact_inventory: one row per product, per region, per day. fact_shipments: one row per shipment. Stating the grain first avoids double counting.

**How do you calculate days of cover?**
Units on hand today divided by average daily sales over the last 30 days. Example: 300 units and 50 sold a day gives 6 days. I guard against division by zero with NULLIF. The forecast-based version for risk uses forecast daily demand in the denominator instead.

**What is the difference between the SQL days of cover and the risk step?**
The SQL KPI looks backward (last 30 days of sales). The risk step looks forward (the 30-day forecast). They can disagree slightly, and the dashboard labels which is which.

**How is stockout rate defined?**
Product-region-days that ended with zero stock divided by all product-region-days. It is [[stockout_overall]]% overall; the worst region is [[worst_region]] at [[worst_pct]]% because of its planted seasonal spike.

**How is on-time delivery calculated?**
Delivered on or before the promised date, divided by shipments whose promised date has already passed. A due shipment that has not arrived counts as late. If I ignored undelivered shipments the bad plant would look better than it is. I use a LEFT JOIN so those rows are kept.

**Why a moving average and not a machine-learning model?**
The goal is a forecast that is explainable and checked, not the fanciest one. A 14-day average is easy to defend, and I proved it beats naive baselines before using it. A model like gradient boosting needs more history, features and tuning, and I would not claim it without measuring it against this baseline.

**How did you validate the forecast?**
Backtest: train on the first part, hide the last 30 days, forecast them and compare with actuals. Error metric is WAPE (total absolute error divided by total actual sales). Moving average [[wape_ma]]%, naive last-day [[wape_naive]]%, seasonal-naive (same weekday last week) [[wape_snaive]]%. On 30-day totals per pair, which is what matters for running out, the error is [[tot_ma]]% against [[tot_naive]]% for naive. Bias is [[bias_ma]]% (negative means it forecasts slightly low). The daily error is mostly random noise in the data, which no model can remove.

**What are the weaknesses of your forecast?**
1) A moving average lags a trend, so in the region with a seasonal ramp it forecasts low. 2) It ignores weekday patterns. 3) Sales are censored by stockouts: when stock is zero you cannot sell, so sales understate true demand and the forecast for the worst-off items is too low. 4) One 30-day backtest window is less robust than repeating the test over several windows.

**How do you decide something is at risk?**
Days until empty = stock today / forecast daily demand. Under [[risk_days]] days is At risk, over [[excess_days]] is Excess. Both thresholds live in a table (kpi_thresholds) so the business can change them in one place.

**How do you explain the "why"?**
Simple rules, not a model: overdue shipments still in transit for that product and region, a supplier with on-time below 70%, or demand up more than 30% versus the prior month. They are checked against the data, so each reason is a fact about that item.

**What would change with real data?**
Real feeds instead of a generator (ERP, warehouse system, logistics tracking); data quality checks (missing days, returns, negative stock); orchestration and scheduling instead of running scripts by hand; incremental loads instead of reloading everything; a seasonal or ML forecast tested against this baseline; demand adjusted for stockout days; lead time per supplier used in the risk threshold instead of a fixed [[risk_days]] days; and access control and audit for pharma compliance.

For example, I can walk you through how inventory is computed, the SQL for days of cover, and how the backtest works." Then be ready to actually do that. Re-read the files on Sunday.
